# Why should an agent believe you?

I run my knowledge through AI agents all day. So do you, probably, or you will soon. And somewhere in that shift, there is a question hiding in plain sight:

When an AI assistant quotes a source, how does anyone know the source actually said that?

Not ”trust me”. Not ”it was in the training data”. Not even ”here's a link” - links rot, pages change, and an agent that fetched something yesterday cannot prove today that it got what it says it got. Picture the ordinary version of the failure: an agent cites an archive, the citation lands in a report, the report gets forwarded, a decision gets made - and the archive never said that. Nobody lied, exactly. There was simply nothing to check. The agentic web is being built on an honor system, and honor systems do not scale.

## The moment the question got real for me

This summer I rebuilt my old podcast archive, 500 episodes of #ensakidag, into something an AI agent can actually use: a public MCP server. Any assistant can search it, read it, and cite it. Lovely. And then the obvious next thought landed like a brick: I have just made it trivially easy for any AI in the world to quote me. What stops any AI in the world from misquoting me?

Nothing, it turned out. So we built something - and then built it twice more, because the interesting problems only show up when the corpora differ. Today, the pattern runs on three servers. **My own archive**, where I am both operator and author. **A book** - 181 texts by 184 authors (some co-written) from a legendary Swedish web gathering in 2010, republished with per-author rights and an AI-reply layer. And **a growing index** of what RISE and AI Sweden, two of Sweden's publicly funded AI organizations, publish about AI - third-party content I index but do not own. Three corpora, three rights situations, one spec.

Every response from all three now carries provenance - origin, rights, freshness, minted-at timestamp - and an Ed25519 signature over the whole payload, verifiable against a public key at a well-known URL with standard libraries. No account needed. Either the math checks out, or it does not. If you know C2PA, the content-credentials standard for images and video, this is the same spirit at a different layer: not "this image came from this camera", but "this answer came from this archive, unaltered".

## Isn't this just TLS?

The most common first objection, so head-on: yes, the pipe is encrypted. TLS proves you are talking to the right server *right now*. It proves nothing five minutes later. An agent queries my archive, saves the answer into its context, its logs, and a report it writes for you. You forward the report. Someone quotes it onward. At every hop after the first, TLS has left the building. A signed payload travels with its own proof: anyone downstream, offline, next year, can verify that this exact answer left this exact server, unmodified, carrying the timestamp of when it was minted. That is the claim, precisely - **portable, offline-checkable attribution**. Not more, not less. (And ”not more” includes replay: a signed answer stays verifiable forever; what the timestamp gives you is honesty about *when* it was true.)

## What the signature proves - and what it never can

Think of it as a ladder. A signature proves the server is who it says it is, the payload is intact, and the provenance is what the operator declared. It *enables* the next rungs - checking source authority, checking that a summary is faithful to what it cited. It never touches the top rung: truth. If I said something wrong in episode 212, the signature faithfully certifies that I said that wrong thing.

The rungs also differ by corpus, which is why building three mattered. For my archive, operator and author coincide - a verified response comes close to ”Joakim's archive said this”. For the RISE and AI Sweden index, the signature proves *my index* served this representation of their material - not that they published those exact words; the provenance says who publishes the originals, on what legal basis I index them, and that copyright sits where it always sat. For the book, rights are per-author, and the provenance says so. Saying exactly what you can prove, and no more, is not a limitation of the approach. It *is* the approach.

Which is also where the genuinely new part lives - not in the cryptography, which is table stakes, but in what sits **inside** the signed scope. The book's AI-written replies each carry a label, in Swedish: "AI-fantasi i författarens anda - INTE författarens egna ord". AI fantasy in the author's spirit, NOT the author's own words. The label is signed with the content. Tamper with the line between what a human wrote and what a machine imagined, and the signature breaks. C2PA already does signed AI-generation assertions for images and video; the contribution here is narrower and, I think, sharper: **tamper-evident honesty labeling for a text corpus served to agents** - rights and human/AI distinctions living inside the signed payload itself.

## The time we signed the wrong label

Credibility scene, at our own expense. Early on, one server's documentation said the signature covered one field while the code signed another. Valid signatures, wrong label. Our verification loop - an independent agent re-checking the live wire against the published claims - caught it; the fix took an hour, and two rules went straight into the spec: scope labels are normative, and documentation describing a live system gets re-verified after every change, because ”still true” is a claim like any other. It happened again, by the way, at spec scale: an external reviewer ran our own published verifier against the live servers and found the gap between the documents and the wire - and the distance from their report to a shipped conformance release was one afternoon. That turnaround, not any single signature, is the actual argument. If you take one thing from this essay, take the loop: build, verify from outside, verify again.

## Nobody verifies yet - and the neighborhood is filling up

No MCP client verifies these signatures automatically today; verification lives with curious humans, gateways, and audit pipelines. But the protocol just made room - the July 2026 MCP spec made extensions first-class, with namespaced metadata as the sanctioned surface, which is exactly where this pattern's envelope sits - and the neighborhood is genuinely active. Cloudflare's Web Bot Auth and RFC 9421 sign the *caller and the pipe*. Google's AP2 signs *payment mandates*, with TTLs, because money taught them about replay. An MCP community discussion on tool bills-of-materials independently converged on the same ingredients - JCS, SHA-256, JWS - for signing tool *definitions*; an IETF draft does the same with ECDSA; a peer-reviewed proposal for a trustworthy MCP registry composes well-known discovery, Sigstore, and JCS/JWS for *registries*; another discussion proposes carrying C2PA credentials in `_meta`; and Sirenic ships a commercial MCP server whose pitch centers on verifying the signature before reading the provenance. Each targets an adjacent layer; none signs the content payload of a knowledge corpus with the rights and honesty labels inside the envelope. Naming the neighbors matters: ”among the first” is only credible when you show you looked - and independent convergence on the same architecture is the strongest signal the problem is real.

One regulatory edge, drawn with care: the EU AI Act's Article 50 now requires machine-readable marking of AI-generated content. A signed human corpus is not Article 50 compliance, and I will not pretend otherwise. But it is the mirror image the law just made visible: as marking machine output becomes mandatory, how do humans mark what *they* made, so the machines can tell? A signed corpus with the AI-content labels inside the signed scope is one working answer to the voluntary half of that question.

## Steal it - and tell me what broke

The spec, test vectors, and a one-command verifier are in the repo; the three servers are the running proof. The pattern is small enough to read in an afternoon and copy in a day. That estimate holds for a specific reason: these servers were built almost entirely by talking to AI, and the same outside-in verification loop that caught our own label bug is what checked every step - the method travels with the pattern.

So, the specific ask, because ”let's compare notes” is what people say when they don't expect mail. **I am looking for three operators - an archive, a museum, a municipality, anyone whose own corpus agents will quote - to run the conformance checklist against their data and report what broke.** Three strangers reproducing this is worth more than any launch post. And if you build MCP clients, the first client that verifies signatures automatically gets to define what the padlock of the agentic web looks like. That prize is still unclaimed.

Why should an agent believe you? Right now, it just does. That is the vulnerability. Making belief checkable, one signed answer at a time, is the repair.
